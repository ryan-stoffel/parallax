Parallax runs coding agents on your own computers and lets you reach them from the desktop app and from your phone. Your threads, prompts, code, and files stay on your computers. This page says what the Parallax services do hold, and what they can't see.

In short

  • A Parallax account holds your email address, your name, and how you sign in.
  • Parallax Connect, the relay that links your devices, keeps a list of your computers, your phones' push tokens, and byte counts. It carries your threads encrypted end to end and can't read them.
  • Notifications carry a thread's title and text sealed with a key only your devices hold.
  • The phone app has no analytics and no advertising code. The desktop app sends anonymous usage counts, which you can turn off.
  • We don't sell your data, and we don't use it for advertising.
  • You can delete your account in the app or on the web.

What stays on your computers

The Parallax program on each of your computers keeps your threads, prompts, agent replies, code, files, settings, and the list of devices you approved. None of it is sent to us. The desktop app and the phone app read it over a connection that is encrypted from your device to your computer.

Your computers talk to the AI providers you signed in to, such as Anthropic, OpenAI, or Cursor, with your own accounts there. Those requests go from your computer to the provider and not through us. Each provider's own terms and privacy policy cover them.

Your account

Accounts are held by Clerk, our sign-in provider. We keep no account database of our own. Your account holds:

  • Your email address.
  • Your first and last name.
  • How you sign in: GitHub, Google, Apple, or an email address with a password, and any passkeys you add.
  • Your profile picture, when the sign-in provider gives one or you set one.
  • A record of your sign-in sessions, which Clerk keeps with each session's device, browser, IP address, and approximate location, so your account page can list them.

When you sign in with GitHub, Google, or Apple, that provider tells Clerk who you are and learns that you signed in to Parallax. If you set a password, Clerk stores it hashed. The apps never see it.

Each app keeps its sign-in on the device, in the system keychain or the operating system's encrypted storage: a token that renews the session, and the last copy of your name, email address, and picture to show in the app.

Parallax Connect

Parallax Connect is a relay at relay.parallax.codes, running on Cloudflare. It lets your phone and your other computers find and reach a computer that isn't on the same network.

What the relay stores

For What
Your account Your account's id from Clerk. The ids of the computers you added, up to 10. The ids of computers you removed, up to 100, so your other devices stop using them.
Each phone The push token Apple or Google gave the app, up to 20 per account. It is what lets the relay send that phone a notification.
Each computer Its id, which is a fingerprint of its public key. The public key. The name you gave the computer, up to 64 characters. Which account it belongs to. When it last connected. The number of bytes it relayed today, counted to cap cost, and replaced each day.
Open connections Random ids for the connections open right now, removed when each one closes.
Webhooks If you make a scheduled task with a webhook, a hash of its address. A request sent to it while your computer is offline is held, with its headers and body, for up to 24 hours and deleted once your computer takes it.
Guest links If you share a thread with a guest link, the link's id, a hash of its pass, when it expires, and the bytes it used today. The link's secret stays in the link and is never sent to the relay.

What the relay can't see

The relay never stores or reads thread content. Your device opens an encrypted connection to your computer through the relay and accepts only that computer's own key, so the relay passes along bytes it can't read or change. That covers your prompts, agent replies, code, files, terminal output, and attachments.

The relay can see which of your computers are online, when a connection opens and closes, and how many bytes it carries. A webhook is different: the service that sends one sends it to the relay over HTTPS, so the relay handles its contents to pass them on.

Logs

The relay writes operational logs to Cloudflare. A log line holds an event's name, a time, and the first 8 characters of a computer's id. It holds no account id, email address, token, or content. Per-request logs are turned off. Cloudflare keeps these logs for TODO (Ryan): Workers Logs retention on the account's plan.

Cloudflare handles every request to the relay and so processes your device's IP address. The relay uses it to limit how many requests one address can send each minute, and doesn't store it.

Notifications

When a thread needs your approval, finishes, or fails, your computer tells the relay, and the relay sends a notification to your phones through Apple's push service or Google's Firebase Cloud Messaging. Apple or Google and the relay see:

  • The phone's push token.
  • A fixed line of text for the event, such as "A thread finished".
  • The ids of the computer and the run, so a tap opens the right thread.

The thread's title and the start of the agent's reply travel sealed. Your computer encrypts them with a key it makes itself, and your phone gets that key directly from your computer over the encrypted connection. The relay, Apple, and Google can't open them. Your phone opens them on the device and shows the fixed line if it can't.

The phone app

The phone app contains no analytics, crash reporting, or advertising code. It asks for these permissions only when you use the feature:

  • Camera and photos, to attach a photo to a message. The photo goes to your computer over the encrypted connection.
  • Microphone and speech recognition, to dictate a message. Speech is turned into text on the phone when the phone supports it. Otherwise the phone's own speech service from Apple or Google does it. Parallax receives no audio.
  • Local network, to find your computers on the same network.
  • Notifications, described above.

The app keeps on the phone: your sign-in, the list of your computers and the keys that prove the phone to them, the notification keys, the names and states of up to 8 recent threads for the widget and Live Activity, your preferences, and a connection log you can read in the app. Signing out removes the sign-in, the recent threads, and the notification keys. Deleting the app removes the rest.

The desktop app

The installed desktop app sends three kinds of usage events to PostHog, so we can count how many installs are in use and how much they do:

  • That the app is in use, at launch and once a day.
  • That a prompt was sent, and whether it started a thread or followed one up. Never its text.
  • When you're signed in, the number of tokens each model used per hour on your computers, and the cost when the provider reports one.

Each event carries the app's version and release channel, the operating system, the processor type, and an id. Signed in, the id is a one-way hash of your account's id. Signed out, it is a random id made for the install. No event carries your name, your email address, a file path, a prompt, or code. PostHog is told to keep no profile for the id and to add no location from the request. PostHog keeps events for TODO (Ryan): event retention on the PostHog project.

To turn this off, set DO_NOT_TRACK=1 in the app's environment. The project's README has the steps.

Other requests the apps make

The apps check GitHub for new releases and download updates from it, so GitHub sees your IP address when they do. This website sets no cookies and runs no scripts or analytics. Cloudflare serves it and processes each request's IP address to do so.

How we use data

We use what's listed above to:

  • Sign you in and keep your devices signed in.
  • Show each of your devices the computers on your account, and connect them.
  • Send the notifications you turned on.
  • Keep the relay's cost and abuse within limits.
  • Count how much the desktop app is used.

We don't sell personal data. We don't use it for advertising or share it with advertisers or data brokers. We don't track you across other companies' apps or websites. We can't use your threads to train AI models, because we never receive them.

How long we keep data

  • Your account stays until you delete it.
  • A computer on the relay stays until you remove it in the desktop app's Settings > Connections. Removing it deletes everything the relay keeps for that computer.
  • A push token stays until Apple or Google reports that it no longer works, or until newer tokens on your account push it past the limit of 20.
  • Held webhooks are deleted when delivered, or after 24 hours.
  • Byte counts are kept for the current day only.
  • Logs and usage events are kept as the Logs and desktop app sections say.

Deleting your account

You can delete your account from the phone app, the desktop app, or the web. The steps are on Delete your account.

Deleting removes your account from Clerk: your email address, name, picture, sign-in methods, and sessions. Every device is signed out. Your threads and files are untouched, because they are on your computers.

The relay doesn't yet learn that an account was deleted. Until that is fixed, it keeps the deleted account's list of computers, its list of removed computers, its push tokens, and each computer's registration. Nobody can sign in to that account again, so nothing can reach that data through the relay. A sign-in token issued before the deletion keeps working at the relay until it expires, which is at most one day. Removing each computer in the desktop app's Settings > Connections before you delete the account clears the computers' records. To have the rest removed, contact us.

Security

Connections to your computers use TLS 1.3 to a key your device pins, whether they go over your network or through the relay. Sign-ins and keys on your devices are kept in the system keychain. Report a vulnerability privately through GitHub's private reporting.
